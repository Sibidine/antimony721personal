Leaving The Nest

Home Musings

Expressing emotional states as metaphors of the various facets of life I’ve witnessed is something I rather enjoy, quite to the chagrin of any poor soul burdened with trying to make sense of my ramshackle incoherence. However, this first week of life in a hostel — nay, an Indian hostel — has been a reality check of such magnitude that my maladies for metaphors have quite seemingly been extinguished. The following may adopt a rather dry tone as compared to the flights of vocabulary and thought my other musings might have.

July 24th morning. Woke up at 6:30 AM, got ready, came out at 7, and saw that registration had begun. I stood in line, got my documentation ratified, and received the keys to my abode for the next year: D-614. Top floor. The heat was going to be bad — I would definitely need a cooler, especially at this time of year in Kanpur. But let’s leave that Chekhov’s gun on the table for now.

It was a double-sharing room, albeit a rather small one. Fewer roommates to please equals fewer headaches, I thought. I dropped my suitcase and went to procure a mattress, pillows, a bucket, and other essentials. Managing that in a horribly organised line under the sweltering sun was nothing short of an immense effort from my father.

As we unpacked, my roommate walked in. Gaurav, three years my senior, hailed from Varanasi and seemed to have lived quite the life already. He was hesitant to get a cooler, so we decided to endure one night and decide the next day. Big mistake.

That night was my first real reality check. As someone accustomed to at least an hour or two of air-conditioning before sleep during the summer, trying to sleep through the Kanpur heat was brutal. I barely slept before the humidity woke me at 4 AM — pillow soaked, shirt drenched. Possibly the worst night of sleep in my memory.

The next day was full of orientations. Cycling in the heat, sitting through standard introductory sessions in a large auditorium — nothing remarkable there. Thankfully, my father, resourceful as ever, managed to secure a cooler at a cut price. That night’s sleep was infinitely better.

We travelled around campus in the pouring rain, exploring shopping complexes and places to eat. Perhaps his impending departure made me cherish his company far more than I otherwise might have.

The following days involved more orientations, long lines for biometrics and ID cards, and bouts of homesickness. As I write this on July 30th, one day before academics begin, all I can think of is taking life one day at a time until I can briefly return home.

What I have left out in the summary above is what truly unsettled me.

On arrival, I saw a clean western toilet with a jetspray. I was overjoyed. Less than 12 hours later, they were overflowing with faeces. The sight disgusts me even now. Hygiene is fundamental, and I feared I would not have it here.

My ever-caring father allowed me to use his guest room facilities for two days. Eventually, I found clean restrooms on the floor below and in my department building. Fingers crossed they remain so.

Water outages were frequent, likely due to overcapacity. Electricity failures followed — MCBs refusing to switch on, floors plunged into darkness. With the warden unavailable, sleepy postgraduates scrambled to coordinate electricians. At 3:30 AM one night, a group of half-awake scholars restored power themselves.

My roommate left midway through the week citing family issues and has yet to return. In his absence, a rodent took residence in my room. After two pilgrimages in its direction, I decided to leave the lights on when away, hoping to discourage its tenancy.

Yet amidst the swamp of homesickness and maintenance issues, there have been rays of hope.

The people here have been nothing short of welcoming. Cleaning staff — prompt and professional. Strangers stopping to check on me after a fall from my bike. Mess operators smiling when I fumbled plates. Seniors offering seats while waiting in line. Hospitality and courtesy that rival expensive hotels, extended purely out of goodwill.

Perhaps even in the advancing darkness of modern cynicism, “atithi devo bhava” still lives.

The food has also been commendable — varied menus, generous vegetables, quality chicken dishes, and consistent standards. Perhaps my affinity for North Indian vegetarian food blinds me to flaws, but food has been far from an issue.

All this may simply be the faltering complaints of a sheltered child, long accustomed to comfort and oblivious to the toil that sustained it. Perhaps these conditions are genuinely testing; perhaps they are merely a reckoning.

In this swirl of emotions, and in hearing well-wishers remind me that this too shall pass, I have realised how privileged I have been. Maybe such exile from comfort is necessary — a tempering of mind, body, and soul for a world that spares no softness.

My parents have been iron-clad pillars throughout — constant calls, patient listening, encouragement, financial freedom to ease discomforts, and occasional arduous trips from Kolkata to Kanpur just to ensure I am well.

Perhaps this is not merely a week of discomfort, but the forging of something sturdier within.
