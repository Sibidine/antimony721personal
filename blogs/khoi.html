Khoi

Home Blog

July 2023

A handful of puffed rice, khoi. Innocuous, everyday food for most people, yet this light, puffy cereal turns into the weight of the world for the soul — a light grasp of the hand transformed into an Atlantean task for the mind. Something akin to a misbegotten key to the deepest cellars buried in the darkest dungeons of my mind; a cacophony of emotions and memories, embroiled in both the hopeless despair of failure at the final hurdle and glorious (if modest) rapture.

At a time of self-imposed containment, at the behest of demons both outside my control and in my own past, the little company I had included not much beyond pillars of examination material, towering like the shafts of the ruined pantheon of shattered dreams and dashed expectations — of mine and of people close to me — the stable, steady, robust furniture (save for the not insignificant number of chairs subjected to the inhumane torture of bearing my overweight self), the gentle ticking of the clock and restless whirring of the fan, gentle yet firm reminders that the hourglass of life has no dams, and that the sands of time, once flown, never return. And the jar of khoi.

The fits of gluttony that fueled invasions of the kitchen cellar were replaced by a modest, transparent jar of this cereal that I had never paid much heed to in the past, standing unflinchingly between my pillars of books.

Not the biggest deal to most people. A healthy option, yes — but far from the groundbreaking, awe-inspiring ignitions that spark the most marketable of fairytales. Yet this unassuming product of rice bore both the whip of regret and the outstretched arm of redemption — a merciless devil driving sinners for eternity in the inner circles of hell, but also a Greek goddess who had returned to find her temple in disarray, yet made it her abode, willing to give all her neglectors a chance at redemption. A stark reminder of the deeds that left me in the pit I dug myself, and a rope dangled down to me in a bottomless well — a chance to make things better, if not right.

Every so often, I withdrew a fistful of this consumable, with the level of khoi left in the jar serving as a barometer of the hard work I was putting in to climb out of the hole I was in, the regular replenishments of the jar reminders of the exponential nature of my trek.

At the close of this tumultuous chapter of my life, these jars of khoi, like many others, were left behind by me as relics that bore no further significance, as I moved on both mentally and physically from those four walls that held my hopes and aspirations for as far back as memory lane is cobbled.

Two years on, though, a fateful encounter with this once-sacred food item in the kitchen — kept as ration by my mother as a companion for my imminent temporary solitary life (under completely different circumstances, fortunately) — dragged me back to those days of monotonous self-imposed captivity, when the only freedom I craved was freedom from the shackles of regret — chains which, though rusted quite a bit, still do not snap today.
